(Slide 1)

OpenCommons Health PIM is the personal health data layer for an interoperable healthcare economy.

The product starts from a simple belief: individuals should be able to own, organize, and permission their health information without handing the whole record to yet another centralized health database.

This is a local-first, standards-aligned platform for personal health information management. It is designed for a future where clinical data, device data, documents, tasks, and AI-enabled workflows can work together while the individual remains the point of control.

(Slide 2)

The tension is clear. Healthcare has APIs now, but patients still do not have a trusted control plane.

The market is enormous, regulated, and increasingly digital. But the person’s data is still fragmented across portals, payers, devices, documents, and local notes.

Portals remain institution-centered. Consumer apps often become another data silo. And partners need standards-based workflows without taking custody of everything.

OpenCommons addresses that gap by putting owner authority at the center of the architecture.

(Slide 3)

The timing matters.

FHIR APIs, SMART OAuth, patient access rules, local identity, and owner approval have created the rails for a new category of application.

The missing layer is patient-owned orchestration.

OpenCommons sits above institutional APIs and below partner applications. It can receive, validate, reconcile, and release data by consent. That allows applications to compete on trust, workflow, and permissioned data use instead of proprietary capture.

(Slide 4)

The product has two sides.

At the core is the owner-controlled Solid Pod: local Solid Community Server, authenticated Pod access, eleven health-domain APIs, RDF and Turtle storage, ShEx validation, and an owner-approved anonymized release boundary.

At the integration edge are Epic SMART and FHIR import foundations, HealthKit Bridge status observability, terminology-coded domains, and preview and reconciliation before data is written to the Pod.

The Pod remains the source of authority. Everything else is an integration, projection, or controlled workflow around that owner-managed record.

(Slide 5)

This is where OpenCommons is different.

Most health apps aggregate personal health information into a service-controlled cloud database, then add consent on top.

OpenCommons begins from the opposite direction. Identifiable personal health information stays in the owner’s Pod. Applications and partners become clients of owner authority.

That creates a strategic opening: partners can build useful health workflows without requiring OpenCommons to become the central custodian of every user’s identifiable record.

(Slide 6)

Trusted data unlocks governed AI health services.

The PIM becomes a personal health cockpit where clinical, device, document, and task signals converge under owner control.

AI can help prepare visits, surface gaps, and organize follow-ups without becoming medical advice.

HealthKit signals can become reviewable context for vitals, sleep, and activity.

And partners can deliver navigation, coaching, research support, and workflow services without taking custody of raw personal health information.

The value is not just data storage. The value is governed action on trusted personal context.

(Slide 7)

The governance layer is what makes the AI story credible.

The Solid Pod decides what AI can observe, summarize, or release.

Consent gates ensure that owner approval, provenance, and purpose bind every external action.

Behavior-shaping engines and deterministic state machines constrain AI into governed workflows.

Auditability makes transitions, diagnostics, and tests inspectable.

This is not unconstrained clinical automation. It is a workflow assistant operating inside a patient-owned data authority and a deterministic control system.

(Slide 8)

The beachhead workflow is Annual Medicare Wellness.

It is high-value, recurring, multi-source, and consent-heavy. It naturally touches the full value proposition.

Before the visit, the owner sees record status and gaps.

During the visit, vitals, medications, conditions, and the care plan can be reviewed.

After the visit, documents, recommendations, and follow-up tasks can be reconciled.

Ongoing HealthKit and owner updates keep the record alive between encounters.

This workflow proves import, reconciliation, storage, explanation, and selective release with the patient in control.

(Slide 9)

For partners, OpenCommons creates a way to build health data workflows without owning the patient.

Health systems can reduce friction in patient-facing import, review, and longitudinal preparation.

Payers can support owner-mediated records and documents that complement patient access strategies.

Device ecosystems can land authorized telemetry into a privacy-preserving personal context.

Research and public-good efforts can receive anonymized, owner-approved release rather than raw personal health information resale.

The partner promise is useful collaboration without default data capture.

(Slide 10)

The commercial model follows the trust posture.

First, design-partner pilots with health systems, payer innovation teams, senior-care networks, and device partners.

Second, AI and integration services: governed workflows, connector work, mapping, validation, deployment, and support.

Third, a personal client tier for individuals and caregivers who want premium local and native experiences without a business model dependent on personal health information resale.

The venture thesis is that governed AI and local-first data custody can unlock regulated services without defaulting to centralized data capture.

(Slide 11)

Defensibility compounds across four layers.

First, trust posture: local-first, owner-mediated, and anonymized by default for external release.

Second, the standards graph: FHIR-style domains, SMART and FHIR, Solid, ShEx, OpenAPI, and terminology systems.

Third, deterministic engines: behavior shaping, source diagnostics, repeatable tests, and constrained actions.

Fourth, workflow memory: preview, reconciliation, provenance, audit, and domain-specific user experience.

This is an execution moat built from trust architecture plus integration depth.

(Slide 12)

The roadmap is disciplined.

Now, keep the localhost MVP stable: eleven domains, Solid Pod authority, OpenAPI documentation, local and container deployment, and owner-mediated workflows.

Next, harden Epic sandbox integration with dynamic JWT, preview diagnostics, and controlled import behavior.

Then, prove the Annual Wellness partner workflow.

After that, expand documents, workflow tasks, HealthKit Bridge observability, and governed AI services.

Only then should the platform scale into native, hosted, and enterprise options.

The goal is to prove workflow, trust, and willingness to pay before taking on public hosted personal health information custody.

(Slide 13)

This is why the wedge matters.

OpenCommons is not another personal health record.

It is a personal health AI operations layer: owner-controlled data, clinical and device context, deterministic behavior shaping, and permissioned outputs that partners can trust because individuals remain in control.

That is the rare overlap venture investors and strategic partners care about: a large regulated market, standards tailwinds, privacy-first AI, and a practical wedge into recurring health workflows.
